Due Inquiry (Domestic Inquiry): Fair Process Before Discipline

A practical briefing for Malaysian employers and HR teams on running a defensible inquiry process before major disciplinary decisions.

Request Domestic Inquiry Program Back to Industry Pulse Training Needs Analysis

When misconduct is alleged, speed is tempting — and risky. In Malaysia, major disciplinary outcomes are far more defensible when the employer can show a fair, documented process: clear allegations, a real chance to respond, and a decision based on evidence — not assumption.

That process is commonly called a domestic inquiry or due inquiry. It is not a court hearing. It is an internal fact-finding step that protects both the organisation and the employee from arbitrary action.

Why it matters

  • Supports fair treatment and consistent application of company policy
  • Creates a paper trail if the decision is later challenged
  • Reduces the risk of managers “skipping steps” under operational pressure
  • Helps HR and line leaders act with the same standard across departments

Core elements of a sound inquiry

1. Clear allegation

The employee should understand what is alleged, in plain language, with enough detail to respond — date, place, and nature of the issue where possible.

2. Opportunity to be heard

A genuine chance to explain, bring relevant points, and respond to the case put forward — not a formality after the outcome is already decided.

3. Impartial panel

Where practical, the panel should not be the same person who investigated or who has a direct conflict that undermines confidence in the process.

4. Evidence-based finding

Notes, documents, and witness accounts should support the finding. Conclusions should match what was actually established in the inquiry.

Common gaps in practice

  • Verbal warnings and “quiet exits” with no written record
  • Managers deciding outcome before the employee is heard
  • Vague charge letters that make a fair response impossible
  • Inquiry notes that are too thin to reconstruct what happened
  • Inconsistent treatment between similar cases in different teams

Practical checklist for HR and managers

  1. Confirm the issue is serious enough to warrant formal process
  2. Issue a clear written notice of the allegation and inquiry details
  3. Allow reasonable time for the employee to prepare
  4. Keep a structured record of proceedings and documents considered
  5. Separate findings of fact from the final disciplinary decision where your policy requires it
  6. Communicate the outcome in writing and file the full pack securely

Training angle

Many workplace disputes escalate not because policy is missing, but because supervisors were never trained to run a calm, consistent process. Short, applied programmes on domestic inquiry help HR and line leaders reduce process risk before a difficult case arrives.
